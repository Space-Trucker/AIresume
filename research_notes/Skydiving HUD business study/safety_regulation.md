# Safety, Regulation, Liability and Human Factors for a Skydiving HUD (AR goggles / smart helmet)

Research date: 2026-09-28. Method note: ~26 web searches. Almost every direct page fetch (uspa.org, ecfr.gov, apf.com.au, skydiveaz.com, dropzone-hosted SIM PDFs) was blocked by the egress proxy, so most findings rest on search-result snippets. Items marked "(snippet)" were not verified against the full primary text. Statements drawn from background knowledge are kept in Inferences or Gaps, never presented as cited facts.

## 1. Skydiving operational rules (USPA, FAA Part 105, BPA, APF, EASA): would a HUD be allowed, including for students?

### Takeaway
No aviation regulator certifies or bans skydiving altimeters or HUDs. FAA Part 105 regulates the parachute assembly (TSO-C23 reserve, rigger packing), not instruments. What actually controls use is governing-body rules and dropzone policy. USPA BSRs require students to wear a visually accessible altimeter and an AAD. Head-mounted gear is treated as a snag hazard, and cameras carry a long-standing (and now disputed) 200-jump guideline. A HUD for students would need governing-body or dropzone approval, and it would likely meet the same "don't let devices replace the student's own altitude-awareness habits" resistance that audible altimeters face.

### Cited Findings
- 14 CFR 105.43: a solo parachutist using a single-harness dual-pack parachute must have a main and an approved reserve; the reserve must be packed by a certificated rigger (105.43(b)) (snippet) — [eCFR Part 105 Subpart C](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-105/subpart-C)
- Reserve parachutes must meet TSO-C23 (personnel parachute assemblies). TSO-C23 sets minimum performance standards for parachute assemblies and components, not instruments (snippet) — [FAA DRS TSO-C23f](https://drs.faa.gov/browse/excelExternalWindow/1A8B18FA46DB168786257A830054D117.0001); [Federal Register TSO-C23d](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2008/12/24/E8-30638/personnel-parachute-assemblies-tso-c23d)
- FAA guidance on sport parachuting is in Advisory Circular AC 105-2E (current) and its predecessor AC 105-2D — [AC 105-2E](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_105-2E.pdf)
- USPA BSR: all students except tandem students must have a visually accessible altimeter and a functional AAD serviced on the manufacturer's schedule. Required student equipment also includes an RSL (snippet) — [USPA SIM 2-1 (BSRs)](https://www.uspa.org/SIM/2-1); [Skydive Chesapeake student guide](https://skydivechesapeake.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/SC_studentguide.pdf)
- USPA members must follow both the BSRs and the FARs. BSR compliance is mandatory for participation in USPA programs (snippet) — [USPA SIM Ch. 2](https://www.uspa.org/SIM/2)
- USPA recommends that jumpers be licensed and have 200 jumps before jumping with a camera "regardless of the camera type, shape or size." This is a SIM recommendation (Section 6-8 in older SIMs; camera flying also appears at SIM 5-7), not a BSR (snippet) — [USPA SIM 6-8](https://www.uspa.org/sim/6-8); [USPA SIM 5-7 Camera Flying](https://www.uspa.org/sim/5-7); [USPA Safety Check "200—The Non-Magical Magic Number"](https://www.uspa.org/safety-check-200the-non-magical-magic-number)
- CONFLICT: one retailer/blog snippet states the SIM 6-8 recommendation to hold a C license (200 jumps) before camera jumping "is no longer valid" (attributed to a 2020 update). USPA's own snippet still says 200 jumps are recommended. The current status could not be verified — [ChutingStar/XDream search snippet via xdsports.uk](https://xdsports.uk/audible-comparison-guide); contrast [USPA SIM 6-8](https://www.uspa.org/sim/6-8)
- Many dropzones enforce the 200-jump camera minimum as house policy. They call a helmet-mounted GoPro a "snag point" and note that a small camera snagging a single line can cause a malfunction — [Skydive Long Island](https://skydivelongisland.com/about/articles/skydiving-with-an-action-camera/); [Skydive California](https://skydivecalifornia.com/blog/why-i-cant-skydive-with-my-gopro/); [Boston Skydive Center](https://bostonskydivecenter.com/blog/bring-gopro-skydiving/)
- UK (BPA): the Operations Manual requires AFF and tandem instructors to jump with an audible altimeter on AFF/tandem descents (Section 6, P4.4). Audibles are generally not recommended for students and FS1 trainees (retailer summary, snippet) — [XDream Skydiving audible guide](https://xdsports.uk/audible-comparison-guide)
- Audibles are "not recommended and often banned" for student skydivers, who need to build their own altitude-awareness habits — [Wikipedia: Pressure altimeter / skydiving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_altimeter)
- Camera-flying guidance says an audible device is essential when flying camera because cameras are distracting. It also says to choose a helmet that fits the audible (snippet) — [XDream Skydiving](https://xdsports.uk/audible-comparison-guide); [APF Camera Flying Guide](https://www.apf.com.au/ArticleDocuments/1260/Camera%20Flying%20Guide.pdf.aspx?EMBED=Y)
- Australia: the APF publishes a separate Camera Flying Guide (content not retrievable; blocked) — [APF Camera Flying Guide](https://www.apf.com.au/ArticleDocuments/1260/Camera%20Flying%20Guide.pdf.aspx?EMBED=Y)
- Canada's CSPA also publishes Basic Safety Rules covering student equipment — [CSPA BSR](https://www.cspa.ca/en/safety/bsr)

### Inferences
- A HUD does not fit the regulatory category of an FAA-approved parachute component. It is an accessory, like altimeters, audibles and cameras, which Part 105 does not certify. No TSO path exists or is needed. (Based on 105.43 covering only the parachute assembly and TSO-C23 covering parachute assemblies.)
- Whether governing bodies treat a HUD as a "camera" depends on the definition. USPA's wording "regardless of the camera type, shape or size" hints that rules key on function (recording, and the distraction and snag risk that come with it), not on "being on the head." A HUD without a camera arguably falls outside camera rules. A HUD with an integrated camera would almost certainly be caught by them, so leaving the camera out of the product is a strategic option.
- Snag risk of helmet protrusions is an established concern. A smart helmet should be low-profile, snag-tested and releasable under load (see AFNOR XP S72-600 in Section 2).
- Student use is the hardest market. Students must already carry a visual altimeter, and instructors resist anything that could replace a student's own altitude-checking habits. A realistic go-to-market targets licensed jumpers first (canopy piloting, wingsuit, coaching, camera flyers), with student or instructor-supervised pilots only through DZ/S&TA agreements.
- Europe (EASA/Italy): parachuting is regulated nationally, by national aero clubs and federations (in Italy, ENAC and the Aero Club d'Italia/FIPS). No evidence was found of any EU-level rule on skydiving instruments. (Not verified; see Gaps.)

### Gaps
- Full, current text of the USPA BSRs and the latest SIM camera section (2025–26 edition) could not be retrieved. Whether a hard BSR camera jump-number rule exists today (versus a SIM recommendation) is unresolved because the sources conflict.
- BPA Operations Manual camera rules (commonly cited as a 200-jump minimum plus a camera-flying requirement) could not be verified from primary text.
- Italian (ENAC / Aero Club d'Italia) and EASA rules for sport parachuting equipment were not found.
- No governing-body statement on HUDs or AR goggles specifically was found.

## 2. Product regulation (EU/Italy, USA, UK, Australia)

### Takeaway
In the EU, a HUD goggle or helmet with GNSS and Bluetooth/Wi-Fi is radio equipment. That triggers the RED, including the RED cybersecurity delegated act that has been mandatory since 1 Aug 2025 (EN 18031-2 covers devices that process location data). It also triggers GPSR (applicable since 13 Dec 2024), the Battery Regulation (replaceable-battery rules from 18 Feb 2027), WEEE and the new Product Liability Directive (applies to products placed on the market after 9 Dec 2026, explicitly covering software). If marketed as protective, the helmet (and possibly the goggles) becomes PPE under Regulation 2016/425. There is a dedicated French skydiving-helmet specification, AFNOR XP S72-600, used under the PPE Regulation, and EN 966 is the air-sports helmet standard. In the UK, the PSTI Act has applied to connectable products since 29 Apr 2024.

### Cited Findings
- RED cybersecurity: Delegated Regulation (EU) 2022/30 (Articles 3(3)(d), (e), (f)) has applied since 1 Aug 2025, postponed from 2024 by Delegated Regulation 2023/2444. It covers wearables, including sports watches — [SGS](https://www.sgs.com/en-se/news/2025/06/red-cybersecurity-requirements-mandatory-on-1-august-2025); [SCHUTZWERK](https://www.schutzwerk.com/en/compliance/red/)
- EN 18031-1/-2/-3 were cited in the OJEU. EN 18031-2 covers radio equipment that processes personal, traffic or location data, including wearables. Presumption of conformity is restricted for some clauses (passwords, parental controls, updates); where these are unmet, a notified body is needed — [SCHUTZWERK](https://www.schutzwerk.com/en/compliance/red/); [CEN-CENELEC](https://www.cencenelec.eu/news-events/news/2025/newsletter/ots-59-cybersecurity-standards/)
- GPSR (EU) 2023/988 applies from 13 Dec 2024, replacing Directive 2001/95/EC. The safety assessment must consider cybersecurity (where it affects safety), later software updates, interconnectivity and "evolving, learning and predictive functionalities" — [Cooley](https://products.cooley.com/2024/12/04/eus-general-product-safety-regulation-an-expanded-concept-of-safety/); [UL Solutions](https://www.ul.com/news/eu-published-general-product-safety-regulation-eu-2023988)
- Product Liability Directive (EU) 2024/2853 entered into force 9 Dec 2024. Transposition deadline is 9 Dec 2026, and it applies to products placed on the market after that date. It covers software (including AI systems), digital manufacturing files and related digital services essential to a product's function. It excludes free/open-source software supplied outside commercial activity — [EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2024/2853/oj/eng); [Nemko Digital](https://digital.nemko.com/regulations/eu-product-liability-directive); [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-product-liability-directive-responding-to-software-ai-and-complex-supply-chains/); [IBA](https://www.ibanet.org/European-Product-Liability-Directive-liability-for-software)
- Battery Regulation (EU) 2023/1542, Art. 11: from 18 Feb 2027, portable batteries in appliances must be removable and replaceable by end users with common tools. There are narrow exceptions, including devices designed for wet environments (replacement by independent professionals allowed) and certain high-cycle-life plus IP67 cases. Commission guidance was published in 2025 (C/2025/214) — [Intertek](https://www.intertek.com/blog/2025/06-27-2027-reqs-for-removability-and-replaceability-of-batteries-in-electrical-products/); [Cooley](https://products.cooley.com/2025/02/13/european-commission-publishes-guidance-on-eu-batteries-regulation-removability-requirements/); [EUR-Lex C/2025/214](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=OJ:C_202500214)
- EN 966 (Helmets for airborne sports; current EN 966:2012+A1:2012) covers construction, chin strap, fastening, field of vision, head mobility, eye protection and penetration resistance. It targets paragliding, hang gliding and ultralights — [SATRA EN 966](https://www.satra.com/ppe/EN966.php); [nlfnorm](https://www.nlfnorm.cz/en/ehn/2046); FAI keeps a [list of EN966 helmets](https://www.fai.org/page/list-en966-helmets)
- All helmets within the PPE Regulation require CE marking. The Tonfly TFX skydiving helmet is certified to EN 966:2012+A1:2012 (HPG category) under Regulation (EU) 2016/425 — [ComplianceGate](https://www.compliancegate.com/helmet-safety-standards-european-union/); [Tonfly manual](https://www.tonfly.com/docs/TFX_User_Manual_multilanguage_xps.pdf)
- AFNOR XP S 72-600 is a parachuting helmet standard developed under Regulation 2016/425. It requires impact testing of all areas at 5.42 m/s with <250 g (hot, cold and aged), snag testing so a parachute line cannot catch on the helmet, and one-handed buckle release under a 50 kg chinstrap load. The Cookie M3 complies — [ChutingStar](https://www.chutingstar.com/blog/m3-joins-impact-rated-helmet-revolution/); [USPA news](https://uspa.org/Information/News/cookie-releases-impact-rated-open-face-helmet); [Cookie Composites](https://www.flycookie.com/shop/skydiving-helmets/m3/)
- UK PSTI Act 2022 and its regulations have applied since 29 Apr 2024. Requirements: no universal or easily guessable default passwords, a published vulnerability-reporting route, and a published minimum security-update period. OPSS enforces, with fines up to £10m or 4% of global revenue plus up to £20k/day — [Ropes & Gray](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/viewpoints/102j653/reminder-new-security-requirements-for-uk-connectable-products-apply-from-29-apr); [Privacy World](https://www.privacyworld.blog/2024/02/the-product-security-and-telecommunications-infrastructure-psti-act-faq/)
- Existing market precedent: Recon Instruments' Flight HUD (goggle-mounted; GPS, altimeter, accelerometer; speed, glide, altitude) sold for skydiving, BASE and wingsuit at $299–$349 (~2012). Deep and Steep's Insight GPS altimeter drives ENGO 2 HUD glasses (altitude, glide, distance to LZ), sold by ChutingStar. AON2's "Project Obsidian" is a visor/goggle HUD altimeter in development — [New Atlas](https://newatlas.com/recon-instruments-gps-goggles-skydiving/24669/); [MIT Tech Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/s/429707/a-heads-up-display-for-skydivers/); [ChutingStar ENGO 2](https://www.chutingstar.com/engo-2-insight-heads-up-display-glasses); [AON2](https://www.aon2.co.uk/project-obsidian/)
- Prior-art patents exist: US5423215A "Self-contained heads-up visual altimeter for skydiving"; US10267630 "Visual altimeter for skydiving" — [Google Patents US5423215A](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5423215A/en); [USPTO 10267630](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10267630)

### Inferences
- PPE status depends on claims. A helmet marketed for head protection is PPE. EU helmets are normally Category II (EU-type examination by a notified body, Module B + C). Some argue that skydiving protects against "consequences that may cause very serious outcomes," which could push toward Category III, but no source was found assessing this. Goggles marketed for eye protection (wind, debris) would also be PPE (likely Cat I–II). A "display-only" smart glass with no protective claim may avoid the PPE Regulation but then falls under GPSR. Recommendation: certify the helmet to EN 966 and/or AFNOR XP S72-600 to meet user expectations for impact rating and snag testing.
- The applicable EU CE stack for the device will likely be: RED 2014/53/EU (radio, safety, EMC via Art. 3(1)(b), cybersecurity via 2022/30), RoHS 2011/65/EU, GPSR (or PPE Reg. if protective), Battery Reg. 2023/1542, WEEE 2012/19/EU, and REACH. Batteries shipped by air also need UN 38.3 testing. The Cyber Resilience Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/2847) reporting obligations begin in 2026 and main obligations in Dec 2027. That date comes from background knowledge and needs verification.
- The EU battery-replaceability rule (Feb 2027) is a design constraint for sealed goggles and helmets. The "wet environment" exception probably doesn't apply to skydiving, so plan a user-swappable pack or a qualifying exemption.
- Under the new PLD, firmware and app updates (including trajectory guidance or dropzone tracking algorithms) are part of the "product," and failure to provide security updates can be a defect. That raises exposure for any safety-relevant display error after Dec 2026.
- USA: Bluetooth/Wi-Fi modules are intentional radiators under FCC Part 15 Subpart C and need equipment authorization (certification via a TCB; a pre-certified module can simplify this). The CPSC has general jurisdiction over consumer products (Section 15(b) defect reporting). There is no CPSC mandatory standard for skydiving helmets; CPSC 16 CFR 1203 applies only to bicycle helmets. (Background knowledge, not searched.)
- Australia: consumer goods fall under the Australian Consumer Law (ACCC), plus RCM marking for EMC/radio via ACMA. (Not searched.)

### Gaps
- Whether a skydiving helmet would be classed PPE Category II or III was not found in any authoritative source.
- No confirmation of the AFNOR XP S72-600 publication year or whether it is being elevated to an EN standard.
- FCC, CPSC and Australian requirements are not verified by searches (budget exhausted).
- No eyewear-standard research (EN ISO 16321, EN 174 ski goggles, ANSI Z87.1) was done.
- Cyber Resilience Act dates are not verified in this session.

## 3. Liability exposure (lawsuits, waivers, insurance)

### Takeaway
No published verdict against an altimeter, AAD or helmet maker was found in this search budget. Visible skydiving litigation targets aircraft/parts makers and operators (e.g., a $48M verdict with $28M punitive damages against an engine-parts maker over a 2006 jump-plane crash). Product liability insurance for protective headgear and extreme-sports equipment is individually underwritten, is noted as higher-premium, and has no published "average" price. Median product liability awards were about $1.5M (2017).

### Cited Findings
- 2006 skydiving plane crash at Sullivan Regional Airport (six killed): a Franklin County jury awarded $48M, including $28M punitive, against an aircraft-engine-parts manufacturer. A separate skydiver crash case settled for $27.5M — [Robb & Robb](https://www.robbrobb.com/in-the-news/2011/april/-48-million-jury-verdict-for-wrongful-death-from/); [AboutLawsuits](https://www.aboutlawsuits.com/skydiving-plane-crash-verdict-17958/); [Robb & Robb settlement](https://robbrobb.com/sky-diver-crash-case-settled-for-27-5-million)
- Wrongful death suits against skydiving operators occur (e.g., a Colson law firm case) — [Colson](https://colson.com/skydiving-company-sued-for-skydivers-death/); plaintiff-firm overview of liable parties (operator, instructor, equipment maker) — [Heidari Law Group](https://www.heidarilawgroup.com/blog/liability-in-skydiving-accidents/)
- All current AADs have had technical issues or misfire reports — [Wikipedia: Automatic activation device](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automatic_activation_device)
- Insurers price sporting-goods product liability by product, sales volume, position in the chain of commerce and geography. Protective headgear is cited as a higher-premium product. Brokers say an "average cost" is not meaningful. The median product liability award in 2017 was $1.5M — [Castle Rock Agency](https://castlerockagency.com/sporting-goods-product-liability-insurance/); [Insurance Canopy](https://www.insurancecanopy.com/product-liability-insurance/manufacturers/sporting-equipment); [products-liability-insurance.com](https://www.products-liability-insurance.com/applications/sports-and-recreational-equipment-product-liability-insurance/)
- Specialty markets (e.g., General Star) write primary and excess liability for sporting goods and athletic equipment makers — [General Star](https://www.generalstar.com/brokerage-insurance/casualty/primary-and-excess-liability/sporting-goods-and-athletic-equipment/)

### Inferences
- Waivers signed at dropzones protect the DZ, not necessarily the equipment maker. US product-liability claims (strict liability, failure to warn) against manufacturers are often not barred by third-party waivers. In the EU, the PLD does not allow contractual exclusion of liability toward injured persons. So the manufacturer's main protections are design (fail-safe, never the primary altimeter), warnings and labelling, the user manual, test records and insurance.
- Positioning the HUD as "supplemental, not a replacement for a primary visual altimeter or AAD" (as audibles are positioned today) will be essential to both governing-body acceptance and liability defense.
- Because the median award (~$1.5M) is small compared with wrongful-death verdicts in aviation ($27–48M), a startup should budget for excess or umbrella coverage. Aviation products exclusions in general liability policies may apply, so a specialty aviation-products or sporting-goods policy is likely needed.

### Gaps
- No specific litigation found against altimeter, AAD or skydiving-helmet manufacturers. That could mean few such cases or limited search reach.
- No quoted premium figures for extreme-sports electronics. The broker pages give no numbers.
- Typical waiver language and its enforceability by country (Italy, UK, Australia) was not researched.

## 4. Human factors: do HUDs help or harm in high-workload tasks? What kills skydivers, and could real-time info help?

### Takeaway
Aviation evidence is mixed but informative. HUDs improve flight-path tracking and detection of expected events. But when a lot of symbology is superimposed on the scene, they impair detection of unexpected events (attentional or cognitive tunneling), and this is exactly the canopy-collision threat. Motorcycle HMD studies show a helmet HUD distracts less than a phone but has not yet shown clear hazard-response benefits. US skydiving deaths are at record lows (9 in 2024, 0.23 per 100k jumps). The leading cause is landing/canopy-control errors, especially low turns, which are problems of judgement and visual attention more than missing data. So a HUD's safety case is plausible for altitude awareness and pattern navigation, but it is unproven and potentially negative for canopy traffic and landing flare.

### Cited Findings
**Aviation HUD research**
- Fadden, Ververs & Wickens (1998) meta-analysis of 18 studies: HUD and conformal benefits included faster detection of symbology changes and traffic and better path tracking. Overall, divided-attention benefits dominated clutter costs. But the benefit depended on the event being expected; costs rose for unexpected, non-salient events — [HFES Proceedings 1998](https://doi.org/10.1177/154193129804200105)
- Fadden, Ververs & Wickens (2001), "Pathway HUDs: Are They Viable?": as more information is added to the HUD, detecting world events costs more, especially unexpected, non-salient events. Attentional tunneling on a pathway HUD inhibited detection of unexpected traffic — [Human Factors 2001](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1518/001872001775900841)
- Wickens & Long (1994): HUD symbology supported precision landing, but pilots were not necessarily processing the symbology and the outside scene at the same time. HUD symbology is perceptually grouped as a separate object — [Semantic Scholar summary](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Attentional-Tunneling-and-Task-Management-Wickens/0344ac49f25c0442dca6de1c75d8c7ed342fec78); [Object-Based Attention and Cognitive Tunneling](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7938696_Object-Based_Attention_and_Cognitive_Tunneling)
- Dowell, Foyle, Hooey & Williams (2002, NASA Ames): cognitive tunneling on non-conformal superimposed HUD symbology could be eliminated by placing the symbology at least ~8° from the out-the-window path being tracked — [HFES 2002](https://doi.org/10.1177/154193120204600125)
- Wickens & Alexander, "Attentional Tunneling and Task Management in Synthetic Vision Displays" — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/254304410_Attentional_Tunneling_and_Task_Management_in_Synthetic_Vision_Displays); related: "Head-Up Displays and Attention Capture" — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/24297897_Head-Up_Displays_and_Attention_Capture)
- Wickens et al. (2009) "Black Swans" in NextGen: display technologies can degrade performance in off-nominal conditions — [Human Factors](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720809349709)
- Automotive: the visual complexity of a car HUD modulates attentional tunneling (Lee et al., 2024) — [Human Factors](https://doi.org/10.1177/00187208231181496); Horrey & Wickens (2004) on clutter, separation and modality in driving — [Human Factors](https://doi.org/10.1518/hfes.46.4.611.56805)
- 2024 TNO study on attentional tunneling in pilots during visual tracking — [TNO](https://publications.tno.nl/publication/34642299/QQzmL5/burg-2024-attentional.pdf); FAA report DOT/FAA/AM-19/13 on HUD/attention — [ERAU/FAA](https://libraryonline.erau.edu/online-full-text/faa-aviation-medicine-reports/AM19-13.pdf)

**Motorcycling HUDs**
- Grahn & Kujala (2018): a smartphone distracts motorcycle riders more than a helmet-mounted HUD — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/328437186_Visual_Distraction_Effects_between_In-Vehicle_Tasks_with_a_Smartphone_and_a_Motorcycle_Helmet-Mounted_Head-Up_Display)
- "Can a Helmet-mounted Display Make Motorcycling Safer?" (IUI 2018; 12 riders, simulator): HMD hazard warnings were effective and rated attractive with lower workload, but no reaction-time or speed-reduction difference versus other warning systems — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3172944.3172963)

**Skydiving altitude awareness and alert modality**
- Continuously monitoring an altimeter is impractical and dangerous because jumpers must track other jumpers to avoid freefall collisions. Intense concentration on formation work causes loss of time and altitude awareness. Jumpers sometimes ignore audible signals to finish manoeuvres or move clear, or fail to notice them while preoccupied — [Wikipedia: Pressure altimeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_altimeter) (secondary source)
- Camera flying is distracting enough that an audible is considered essential — [XDream Skydiving](https://xdsports.uk/audible-comparison-guide)

**Fatality data**
- 2024: 9 US civilian skydiving fatalities, the first single-digit year since records began in 1961. That is 0.23 per 100,000 jumps (1 in 431,111) out of ~3.88M jumps. The most common cause is landing issues, including intentional and unintentional low turns. The improvement is credited to equipment, training, emergency response and safety culture — [USPA 2024 Fatality Summary](https://www.uspa.org/a-milestone-in-safetythe-2024-fatality-summary); [USPA press release](https://www.uspa.org/us-skydiving-fatalities-hit-record-low-in-2024); [AVweb](https://avweb.com/features/u-s-skydiving-fatalities-reach-historic-low-in-2024/)
- Historic averages: over 20 deaths/yr in the 2000s–2010s, over 30/yr in the 1980s–90s, 42.4/yr in the 1970s — [AVweb](https://avweb.com/features/u-s-skydiving-fatalities-reach-historic-low-in-2024/)
- 2023: 10 fatalities in ~3.5M jumps (~0.27/100k). No student fatalities (only the second such year since 1961). Low turns caused ~25% of 2023 fatalities (secondary blog quoting USPA) — [USPA 2023 Fatality Summary](https://www.uspa.org/a-widespread-improvementthe-2023-fatality-summary); [Skydive Fundamentals](https://www.skydivefundamentals.com/blog/skydiving-fatalities-2025)
- USPA also publishes non-fatal incident summaries. The 2024 Part One covers landing incidents — [USPA 2024 Non-Fatal Incident Summary](https://www.uspa.org/contact-and-about-uspa/uspa-news/the-2024-non-fatal-incident-summary-part-one-landing-incidents)

**GPS/sensor performance at freefall speeds**
- FlySight (the de facto skydiving GPS) reports velocity accuracy of ~0.1 m/s but has ~1 s lag like any GPS. It logs at 5 Hz. FlySight 2 adds barometric, accelerometer, gyro and orientation sensors — [SkydiveNow FlySight](https://skydivenow.co.uk/flysight-gps.html); [ChutingStar FlySight](https://www.chutingstar.com/skydive/flysight-audible-gps)
- GPS (geometric) altitude differs consistently from barometric altitude by ~5–10% in FlySight data. Skydiving altimeters use barometric altitude — [FlySight wiki](http://flysight.ca/wiki/index.php/Configuring_FlySight); [Paralog: Using GPS](https://www.paralog.net/using-gps.html)
- Speed skydiving competitions use GPS for measurement (context for GPS use at >400 km/h) — [Wikipedia: Speed skydiving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_skydiving)

### Inferences
- **Where a HUD could plausibly help:** (a) altitude awareness in freefall, especially for camera flyers, wingsuiters and freeflyers, who can't easily look at a wrist altimeter; (b) canopy navigation and pattern (distance to LZ, wind drift, glide); (c) giving a consistent altitude reference for the turn to final, which could address "unintentional low turns." These are credible hypotheses. No study was found showing outcome benefits in skydiving.
- **Where it could harm:** (a) canopy collisions and traffic avoidance, which are unexpected, low-salience events, exactly where the aviation literature shows HUD costs; (b) landing flare, where eyes must be on the ground and horizon; (c) intentional low-turn performers, who might use speed and altitude readouts to push margins further (risk compensation); (d) students, who may lose the habit of checking their own altimeter; (e) snag and cutaway hazards from protruding hardware; (f) night or low-light glare, fogging and occlusion of the visual field.
- **Design implications from the evidence:** keep symbology minimal and declutter automatically by flight phase. Use conformal or peripheral placement (at least ~8° offset from the primary visual path per Dowell et al.). Use multimodal alerts, with audio as primary for critical altitudes, since audibles are already the accepted backup. Blank or minimize the display below a set altitude on final and during flare. Never present the HUD as the primary altimeter.
- **Latency requirement (inference):** at terminal velocity (~55 m/s belly, ~80+ m/s head-down), a 1 s GPS lag equals 55–80+ m of altitude error. GPS altitude alone is therefore unsuitable for deployment-altitude cues. Barometric altitude, with pressure-lag compensation, fused with IMU data is required. GPS is acceptable for horizontal navigation and canopy glide, where speeds are ~10–20 m/s. A target of end-to-end altitude display latency under ~250 ms would keep error below ~20 m in freefall. This is a derived target, not a sourced standard.
- **Fatality base rate:** with ~9–10 US deaths a year spread across many causes, no device can demonstrate a statistically measurable fatality reduction in the short term. Safety claims in marketing would be unsupported and increase liability. Market on awareness and performance, not "saves lives."

### Gaps
- Full 2024 cause breakdown (counts for canopy collision, low turn, no-pull or low-pull, malfunction handling) was not retrievable (USPA site blocked).
- No peer-reviewed study of HUDs or AR goggles in skydiving, skiing or cycling was found within budget. The Recon ski-goggle era produced little outcome research that surfaced.
- No experimental study comparing audible versus visual altitude alerts in skydiving was found.
- No published latency or accuracy standard for skydiving altimeters (visual or audible) was found.

## 5. Data/privacy and app-store policies

### Takeaway
Continuous GPS tracks and dropzone tracking are personal data under GDPR (and processed by radio equipment within EN 18031-2's scope). Consent or another lawful basis, data minimisation on granularity and frequency, and transparent notices are required. EDPB guidance urges collecting location data only when necessary. Separately, the UK PSTI and EU RED security rules apply to the device.

### Cited Findings
- GDPR applies to any organisation processing personal data of individuals in the EU regardless of location. Wearables collecting personal data are in scope — [GDPR Local](https://gdprlocal.com/gdpr-for-wearable-technology/)
- The EDPB advises that location data be collected only where necessary. Consent-based collection must meet GDPR standards (freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous). Geolocation granularity and frequency should be proportionate to the purpose — [EDPB Guidelines 04/2020](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/our-work-tools/our-documents/guidelines/guidelines-042020-use-location-data-and-contact-tracing_en); [EDPB Connected Vehicles Guidelines 01/2020](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/sites/default/files/consultation/edpb_guidelines_202001_connectedvehicles.pdf)
- EN 18031-2 (RED cybersecurity) specifically addresses radio equipment processing location data, including wearables — [SCHUTZWERK](https://www.schutzwerk.com/en/compliance/red/)

### Inferences
- Live dropzone tracking (sharing jumper positions with other jumpers or the DZ) creates a controller/processor question with the DZ, requires data-sharing agreements, and needs default-off sharing. Retaining jump logs with biometric data (heart rate) could reach GDPR Art. 9 health-data rules.
- App stores: digital subscriptions unlocked inside iOS/Android apps must generally use Apple/Google in-app purchase (with regional exceptions, e.g., the EU DMA and US anti-steering rulings). Hardware sales and physical goods are exempt. Not verified in this session.
- US: state privacy laws (e.g., California CPRA, which treats "precise geolocation" as sensitive personal information) apply. Not searched.

### Gaps
- App Store / Google Play subscription policy and current (2026) anti-steering status were not researched (budget).
- US state privacy law specifics and Australian Privacy Act reforms were not researched.
