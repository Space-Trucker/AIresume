# Launch Glasses First, Retrofit Helmets Later

A skydiving HUD line can be a viable business, but only as a lean niche business, and only if the four-SKU plan is cut down. It will not be venture-scale on skydiving alone. The realistic buyer pool is the **15,000–25,000 wingsuit, canopy, tracking, camera and instructor jumpers** who already pay about $300–$440 for a single electronic instrument. That caps serviceable demand at about **$2–4M a year**. The base case in this study sells about **2,400 units over three years** for about **$1.5M in cumulative revenue**, and gross profit does not cover a small team's operating costs until year 3 or later. The recommended structure is one shared electronics "pod" in two tiers. It launches first as a glasses/goggle product: **Standard at $449 and Premium at $749**, which undercuts today's roughly $1,000 two-device Insight plus ENGO 2 setup. The helmet versions should ship later as **retrofit visor modules at the same prices**, developed with an established helmet maker. A full smart helmet ($1,099 / $1,449 if built) should wait until the glasses have proven demand. Subscription revenue will be small, **under 5% of revenue by year 3**. Membership should therefore be priced like Garmin or Oura (**Pro $69/yr**), with every in-flight safety function kept free. The product must be advisory-only, use barometric plus IMU altitude fusion, ship without a camera, and be restricted to B-licence holders and above. It also has to meet EU rules that bind before first shipment: the Product Liability Directive for products placed on the market after 9 Dec 2026, and user-replaceable batteries from 18 Feb 2027. Estimated cash needed to reach first shipment is **about $0.9–1.9M**. Confidence is moderate on price benchmarks and low on volumes: almost every figure in the underlying research came from search-engine snippets rather than full primary documents, and every BOM, NRE and volume number below is an analyst estimate.

## A 15–25k-jumper niche caps skydiving HUD revenue near $2–4M a year

The US anchors the market. USPA reports **more than 42,400 members and 220+ affiliated dropzones** ([USPA 2025 Fatality Summary](https://www.uspa.org/uspa-news/a-need-for-vigilance-the-2025-fatality-summary)). US jump volume reached **3.88M in 2024** ([AVweb](https://avweb.com/features/u-s-skydiving-fatalities-reach-historic-low-in-2024/)) and then fell to about **3.47M in 2025**, a drop of roughly 10% per a secondary summary of USPA data ([Skydive Fundamentals](https://www.skydivefundamentals.com/blog/skydiving-fatalities-2025)). Other national federations are an order of magnitude smaller. British Skydiving has about **6,400 full members and 28 dropzones** ([British Skydiving](https://britishskydiving.org/overview/)). Germany's DFV reports "over 6,000" members ([DFV](https://www.dfv.aero/DFV)). France's FFP logged **505,576 jumps in 2024, down about 41,000 year on year**, its second consecutive large decline ([Paramag](https://paramag.fr/activite-ffp-2024/)). No global census exists. The oft-quoted "70,000 skydivers worldwide" is stale. Building up country by country gives about **90,000–130,000 active licensed sport jumpers**, and this study uses 100,000 as the base case. That figure is an inference, not a published number.

The population is flat to shrinking, so growth has to come from attach rate, not head-count. Buyers are engaged, though. In USPA's 2024 survey, **32% of members bought new gear** (29% in 2023), and **31% do tracking and 23% do angle flying**. Those are the disciplines that value glide and trajectory feedback ([USPA 2024 survey via Skydive Fundamentals](https://www.skydivefundamentals.com/blog/skydiving-fatalities-2025)). The average member has about 750 jumps, so the base skews experienced, which suits a product that should be restricted to licensed jumpers anyway.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| TAM (sport) | ~100k active licensed jumpers worldwide × ~$500 average HUD price; 4-year replacement cycle | ~100k people | ~$50M installed base, **~$12M/yr** | Low (head-count inferred) |

| SOM (years 1–3) | 5–10% of SAM | **~1,000–2,500 units cumulative**, ~300–1,000/yr | ~$0.5–1.5M cumulative hardware | Low |
| Adjacent (optional) | Paragliding/speedflying; Germany's DHV alone has ~39–40k members | plausibly 2–4× skydiving head-count | Not sized; strong incumbents (varios, flight computers) | Very low |

The military figure rests on a documented, long-standing need rather than on a price list. The US Navy's HAHO-PNS combined a helmet-mounted navigation pod with a goggle microdisplay and sought a manufacturer for **2,000 systems** ([GlobalSecurity HAHO-PNS](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/systems/aircraft/systems/haho-pns.htm)). The Army's MFF NAVAID integrates with the JPADS Mission Planner ([GlobalSecurity MFF NavAid](https://globalsecurity.org/military/systems/aircraft/systems/mff-navaid.htm)). The paragliding figure comes from DHV's membership ([de.wikipedia DHV](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutscher_H%C3%A4ngegleiterverband)). Neither adjacent market belongs in the consumer SOM, but both are real options if skydiving demand disappoints.

Two data quality warnings matter here. First, the widely circulated "$2.89B wingsuit market" figure comes from market-research press releases and is not credible when set against a few thousand active wingsuit pilots ([VerifiedMarketReports](https://www.verifiedmarketreports.com/product/wingsuit-flying-market/)). Second, the USPA licence-level distribution, which would pin down the SAM, sits in a survey PDF the research could not open. Refining the SAM with that PDF is the cheapest high-value research step left.

## One bolt-on setup, two dead HUDs and one stalled pre-order define the competitive field

No company sells a dedicated, integrated skydiving HUD today. The only shipping option is a bolt-on. Deep and Steep's **Insight GPS altimeter (about €589)** drives **ENGO 2 "Insight HUD Glasses" ($349)**, which show up to three data points such as altitude, glide and distance to landing zone ([SkydiveShop Insight](https://skydiveshop.com/products/insight-gps-altimeter); [Deep and Steep shop](https://deepandsteep.io/shop/engo-2-photochromatic-glasses/); [Deep and Steep HUD Connect](https://deepandsteep.io/knowledge/heads-up-display-hud-connect/)). The combined setup costs roughly **$1,000**; this and all other currency conversions in the report are approximate. The vendor already gates the glasses to jumpers holding **at least a USPA B-licence** ([SkydiveShop ENGO 2](https://skydiveshop.com/products/engo-2-hud-glasses-deep-steep-insight)). SkydiveShop's 2026 altimeter roundup calls Insight "HUD-glasses ready" but adds that "not everyone needs that feature" ([SkydiveShop 2026 roundup](https://skydiveshop.com/pages/best-digital-skydiving-altimeters-2026)). The category exists, but dealers treat it as a niche add-on.

The graveyard is instructive. Recon's 2012 Flight HUD goggle took pre-orders at **$299–$349** and would only be built after 250 pre-orders ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2012/10/19/84972/a-heads-up-display-for-skydivers/); [New Atlas](https://newatlas.com/recon-instruments-gps-goggles-skydiving/24669/)). Reportedly only **616 units** were made before Intel shut Recon down in 2017 ([Recon Instruments, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recon_Instruments)). AO(N²)'s Project Obsidian is priced at **£899**, promised shipping in "summer 2023", and was still listed as a pre-order when searched ([AON2 product page](https://www.aon2.co.uk/products/project-obsidian/)). Its first prototype was "too dim to see in bright sunlight" ([AON2 Project Obsidian](https://www.aon2.co.uk/project-obsidian/)). Dekunu One, a roughly $390 smart altimeter praised for its cloud 3D replay, went **"on hold" in 2022** after complaints about battery and sync ([Dekunu On Hold](https://help.dekunu.tech/article/301-dekunu-on-hold); [Skydivemag review](https://www.skydivemag.com/new/2018-12-12-dekunu-one-review/)).

This landscape points to one position: **the first standalone, single-device skydiving HUD that actually ships and stays supported**. Its competitive advantage is integration: no separate altimeter needed, and baro/IMU/GNSS built into the eyewear. It should also export data in FlySight-compatible formats, so it slots into Skyderby and Jumplog workflows rather than fighting them. The HUD concept itself cannot be defended by patents. A heads-up skydiving altimeter was patented in the 1990s ([US5423215A](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5423215A/en)). Any durable advantage has to come from execution, reliability, dealer relationships and data.

## Price the glasses at $449 and $749, match helmet modules, and keep safety free

Skydivers already accept **$350–$440 for one electronic instrument**. Buyers have repeatedly shown they will pay about $300–$400 for data-rich hardware (FlySight 2, Dekunu One, Recon, ENGO). £899 (Obsidian) has never been tested in the market. A standard SKU at **$449** sits just above the instrument band, which is justified because it combines an altimeter-class sensor stack with a display. It also sits close enough to feel like "one more instrument". A premium SKU at **$749** is 1.67× the standard price, within the **1.3–2× premium/standard ratio** seen across wearables (Whoop Life/One is 1.8×; waveguide/birdbath AR glasses medians are 1.6×) ([AR Compare](https://www.arcompare.com/guides/birdbath-vs-waveguide/); [Whoop](https://www.whoop.com/us/en/membership/)). At $749 the premium SKU is still **about $250 below the Insight + ENGO combination** and well below Obsidian.

The companion app needs a clear freemium boundary. Garmin's **Connect+ ($69.99/yr)** drew a boycott post with about 10,000 upvotes because it appeared to paywall features owners felt they already had ([Tom's Guide](https://www.tomsguide.com/wellness/smartwatches/garmin-launches-a-paywall-here-are-all-the-premium-connect-features-that-will-cost-you-usd6-99-a-month)). For a safety-adjacent instrument that backlash would be worse. Every in-flight display function, firmware update and security patch, and a basic logbook must be free forever. The research found only thin precedent for paid skydiving software: Paralog sells an annual licence, and Jumplog puts FlySight/ProTrack auto-import in its top tier ([Paralog](https://www.paralog.net/purchase.php); [Jumplog](https://www.jumplog.net/)). That supports charging for analysis and automation, not for data access.

Subscriptions sold inside iOS or Android apps generally carry app-store fees, while hardware sales do not. That rule is background knowledge the research did not verify for 2026. Selling Pro through the web account is the safer default.

## Unit economics clear 60% at DTC, but dealers and a full helmet squeeze them

No public BOM exists for a low-volume sports HUD. The best anchor is TechInsights' teardown of the Meta Ray-Ban Display, where **display-related parts are 50.8% of the BOM** ([TechInsights](https://www.techinsights.com/blog/meta-ray-ban-display-teardown-reveals-more-meets-eye)). The optical engine is therefore the line item to control. Small micro-OLED panels retail at **$56–$169**, or $360–$495 with a driver board ([Panox Display](https://www.panoxdisplay.com/knowledge/what-is-the-typical-price-of-a-micro-oled-display.html)). ActiveLook's roughly 9 g HUD module already powers ENGO 2, but OEM pricing requires a quote ([ActiveLook](https://www.activelook.net/pages/the-tech)). Buying such an engine removes an estimated $250k+ of custom-optics NRE in exchange for a higher unit cost. The BOM figures below are analyst estimates for 1,000–5,000 units. They sit inside the research's modelled ranges ($90–$200 standard, $200–$350 premium, $300–$500 helmet).

These prices sit at about **3.1–3.5× BOM**, inside the consumer-electronics rule of thumb of **2.5–4× BOM** ([Bolt](https://blog.bolt.io/hardware-retail-exits/)). DTC margins land near the **65–75% target** that agencies recommend for DTC hardware ([Eightx](https://eightx.co/blog/what-is-keystone-markup)). They are also at or above Garmin's **Fitness (60%) and Outdoor (64–66%) segment gross margins** ([Garmin FY2025](https://www.garmin.com/en-US/newsroom/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/2025_Q4_Earnings_Press_Release.pdf)). The dealer discount is the biggest unknown. Skydiving dealers say gear is "already priced at a very slim margin" and that manufacturers set prices ([ChutingStar](https://www.chutingstar.com/blog/the-truth-on-skydiving-gear-equipment-pricing/)). The 30% dealer and 45% distributor discounts used here are assumptions to confirm with two or three retailers. At a 65/35 DTC/dealer mix, the blended gross margin is about 61%. After a warranty reserve of **3–5% of revenue** (above the 1.52% consumer-electronics claims average, because this is a first-generation product used in a harsh environment ([Warranty Week](https://www.warrantyweek.com/archive/ww20240711.html))) and electronics returns of 8–20% ([ShipNetwork](https://www.shipnetwork.com/post/return-rates-by-industry); [Claimlane](https://www.claimlane.com/resources/blog/electronics-returns-warranty-claims)), the hardware margin is about 52–53%. Payment fees and fulfilment reduce the contribution to about 45–50%.

The helmet question is decided by NRE and risk, not by margin. A full helmet adds an estimated **$100–300k** for shell and liner tooling plus impact certification. It also puts the company in competition with Cookie and Bonehead. AON2 chose glasses so they would fit "most full-face skydiving helmets, including the new impact-rated models", saying it is "not in the business of making skydiving helmets" ([AON2](https://www.aon2.co.uk/project-obsidian-helmet-or-glasses/)). A retrofit module reuses the glasses pod, adds only mount tooling and snag testing, and turns helmet brands into channel partners.

Certification for a BLE/GNSS wearable costs tens of thousands of dollars, not hundreds of thousands. FCC certification with a pre-certified module runs **$3k–$10k**, and pre-compliance testing at **$3k–$8k** cuts first-pass failures (which cost $5k–$30k and 4–12 weeks) from about 50% to under 10% ([MarkReady](https://markready.io/learn/fcc-certification-cost)). UN38.3 battery testing starts at about **$2,000** ([Experior Labs](https://experiorlabs.com/un38-3-lithium-battery-test/)). Soft aluminium tooling costs **$3k–$12k per part** ([Xinyang MFG](https://xinyangmfg.com/low-volume-injection-molding-cost-hardware-startups/)). Contract manufacturers usually want a **30% deposit with 70% paid before shipment**, and the deposit-to-cash cycle can exceed 8 months ([Berkeley Sourcing Group](https://www.berkeleysg.com/cash-flow-is-a-hardware-startup-killing-business/)).

## Three-year revenue reaches $0.8–4.1M, and subscriptions stay under 5%

The scenarios assume first shipments in 2028. The research estimates **12–24 months from concept to shipping** for a module-based design, so shipping before or at PIA in February 2027 is not realistic. All scenarios use a net hardware average selling price of about $520–$540 after dealer mix. They assume accessories (swappable batteries, lenses, mounts, cases) at 8% of hardware revenue and Pro attach of 15–25% of the installed base at $69/yr. Hardware gross margin is 52% after returns and warranty; subscriptions carry 85%.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

These numbers say three things. The base case already needs about 10% of the SAM and sits at the top of the SOM range, so it is not conservative. Base-case gross profit of about **$380k in year 3** is less than an assumed lean team's opex of about $450–650k a year, so the business does not recover its NRE within three years unless it reaches the bull case. The bull case depends on markets beyond sport skydiving. Membership in the base case brings in only about **$70k in year 3** (Pro about $26k, Coach about $14k, Dropzone about $30k). That is why the subscription should be treated as a retention and differentiation tool, not as the revenue engine. Hardware-linked apps can convert better than Strava's roughly 2–3% implied free-to-paid rate, a back-of-envelope figure from its **$415M 2025 revenue** ([Business of Apps](https://www.businessofapps.com/data/strava-statistics/)). Even so, on a base of 2,400 devices the dollars stay small. The one recurring line that could grow is the **Dropzone tier**, because it is B2B and several hundred dropzones worldwide could each pay four figures a year.

## Advisory-only design and EU deadlines shape the product before PIA 2027

No aviation regulator certifies or bans skydiving instruments. FAA Part 105 covers the parachute assembly and TSO-C23 covers reserves, not displays ([eCFR Part 105](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-105/subpart-C)). Acceptance is therefore decided by governing bodies, dropzones and liability. USPA requires students to carry a visually accessible altimeter ([USPA SIM 2-1](https://www.uspa.org/SIM/2-1)). Audibles are "not recommended and often banned" for students so that they build their own altitude-checking habits ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_altimeter)). Cameras carry a 200-jump recommendation, and many dropzones enforce it because head-mounted gear is a snag point ([USPA SIM 6-8](https://www.uspa.org/sim/6-8); [Skydive California](https://skydivecalifornia.com/blog/why-i-cant-skydive-with-my-gopro/)). Sources conflict on whether that recommendation still stands, so dropzone house rules are what count in practice.

The human-factors evidence argues for restraint. Aviation meta-analyses find that HUDs improve path tracking and the detection of expected events. They also find that cluttered symbology impairs detection of **unexpected, low-salience events**, known as attentional tunnelling ([Fadden, Ververs & Wickens 2001](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1518/001872001775900841)). NASA work found tunnelling disappears when symbology sits **at least ~8° from the primary visual path** ([Dowell et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1177/154193120204600125)). Canopy collisions and flare timing fall squarely in the category of unexpected events. The most common cause of US fatalities is landing problems, including low turns ([USPA 2024 Fatality Summary](https://www.uspa.org/a-milestone-in-safetythe-2024-fatality-summary)). With only **9 US deaths in 2024 (0.23 per 100k jumps)**, no device can show a measurable reduction in fatalities, so any "saves lives" marketing would be unsupported and would add liability exposure.

Sensor physics rules out GPS-only altitude. GPS lags by about 1 s, and GPS altitude differs from barometric altitude by about 5–10% ([SkydiveNow](https://skydivenow.co.uk/flysight-gps.html); [FlySight wiki](http://flysight.ca/wiki/index.php/Configuring_FlySight)). At freefall speeds of 55–80 m/s, a 1 s lag is 55–80 m of altitude error. The product therefore needs **baro-primary altitude fused with IMU data**, lag compensation, and an end-to-end display latency target of about 250 ms. That target is derived in this study, not taken from a published standard. GNSS then handles horizontal navigation, glide and dropzone tracking under canopy, where speeds are only 10–20 m/s.

These constraints translate into specific design choices. The product should be labelled and marketed as a supplemental, advisory display that never replaces the primary visual altimeter, the audible or the AAD. Symbology should be minimal, phase-aware and placed in the periphery. Audio should be the primary channel for critical altitude alerts, pairing with or emulating an audible. The display should blank or reduce to one number below a set altitude on final and during the flare. The product should ship **without a camera**, so it stays outside camera rules and adds no recording-related distraction. It should be sold to B-licence holders and above, following Deep and Steep's precedent, with student use allowed only through dropzone/S&TA-supervised programmes. It should be offline-first, with no third-party runtime licence that could disable it. Hardware must be low-profile and snag-tested, and any helmet mount must stay releasable under load.

The EU regulatory calendar binds the product before its first unit ships.

Some of these rules favour the recommended structure. A user-swappable battery pack satisfies the Battery Regulation and doubles as an accessory revenue line. The retrofit module avoids making any PPE claim itself, but it must not undermine the host helmet's certification. The module therefore needs mounting and snag validation carried out jointly with the helmet maker, which is another reason to partner rather than build. On liability, dropzone waivers protect the dropzone rather than the manufacturer. Skydiving verdicts against suppliers have reached **$48M, including $28M punitive**, in an aircraft-parts case ([AboutLawsuits](https://www.aboutlawsuits.com/skydiving-plane-crash-verdict-17958/)), and protective headgear is written as a higher-premium insurance class ([Castle Rock Agency](https://castlerockagency.com/sporting-goods-product-liability-insurance/)). Binding specialty product-liability and excess cover is a precondition for launch, and no premium quotes were found.

## Wingsuit and canopy pilots first; PIA 2027 is a validation event, not a launch

The beachhead is the group that already pays for real-time flight feedback: wingsuit pilots, canopy pilots and swoopers, tracking and angle flyers, and coaches. FlySight 2 is stocked by essentially every specialist retailer, and its marketing leans on a Tony Suits chief test pilot and a competitive swooper ([SkydiveShop FlySight 2](https://skydiveshop.com/products/flysight-2-audible-gps)). Wingsuit coaches already teach FlySight workflows ([Taya Weiss](https://tayaweiss.com/wingsuit-resources/2017/11/introduction-to-flysight)). The HUD should copy that playbook. The plan is two or three sponsored wingsuit and canopy coaches, rewarded with gear and revenue share rather than large stipends, plus a presence at the big wingsuit and swoop hubs and their dropzone pro shops (Perris, Eloy, DeLand, CSC). Camera flyers are a second segment: an audible is considered essential when flying camera ([XDream Skydiving](https://xdsports.uk/audible-comparison-guide)), and the HUD gives them altitude awareness without looking away from the subject. At FAI wingsuit events the judges supply the logging device and competitors may not interfere with it ([FAI 2025 rules](https://www.fai.org/sites/default/files/isc/documents/2025/2025_isc_cr_wingsuit_flying_v2.pdf)). The competition angle is therefore a training tool with Skyderby-compatible export, not an official device.

Distribution is concentrated, which helps a small company. A handful of specialist retailers (ChutingStar, SkydiveShop, Sky-Shop EU, DZ Sports UK, ParaFunalia, Wingstore, Basik) plus dropzone pro shops reach most buyers ([Sky-Shop](https://sky-shop.eu/flysight-audible-gps); [DZ Sports](https://www.dzsports.co.uk/Flysight-gps); [Wingstore](https://wingstore.aero/en/product/engo-2-hud-glasses/)). Retailer comparison articles drive discovery. The **PIA Symposium on 8–11 Feb 2027 in Cincinnati**, with 80+ exhibiting manufacturers, riggers and dropzone owners ([Skydivemag](https://www.skydivemag.com/new/pia-symposium-returns-to-cincinnati-in-2027/)), comes four months from now. It should be used to show working prototypes, collect dealer letters of intent, run a seminar on HUD human factors that invites S&TA scrutiny, and take **refundable** deposits only. Because PIA is biennial, commercial launch would follow at a spring 2028 season opening, and the helmet module could debut at PIA 2029.

Public crowdfunding is a poor fit. The only comparable niche precedents are vendor pre-orders (Recon, AON2, Dekunu's Explorer Edition), and they are associated with delays and damaged trust. Kickstarter technology projects fund only about **20%** of the time, and **61–84%** of funded projects ship late ([BoostYourCampaign](https://www.boostyourcampaign.com/blog/crowdfunding-success-rate-by-category-2026); [Kickstarter](https://www.kickstarter.com/blog/is-lateness-failure)). Dealer and dropzone pre-orders after a live demo are the better way to fund the first production deposit.

The military is an option, not a plan. The need is documented, but the programmes involve low-thousands of units, long procurement cycles, and requirements such as NVG compatibility, JPADS integration and secure GPS, which a consumer product will not meet without a dedicated variant. The US Army has solicited an MFF handheld navigation device ([GovTribe](https://govtribe.com/opportunity/federal-contract-opportunity/military-freefall-mff-parachutist-handheld-navigation-device-w911qyfy13a01)), and incumbents such as Airborne Systems' jTrax already serve the space ([Airborne Systems](https://airborne-sys.com/personnel-parachute-systems/)). The right approach is to pursue a small evaluation contract from year 2, once consumer units show field reliability, and to keep it out of base-case forecasts.

## Recommendation, kill criteria and a 12-month validation plan

Build the business, but not the four-SKU plan as drawn. Engineer **one electronics pod in two tiers**, and launch the **Premium glasses/goggle first** (in 2028, $749, first year of Pro included) to the wingsuit, canopy and coaching segment. The standard glasses at $449 should follow within six months, once early field data supports a lower-cost display. Convert both helmet SKUs into **retrofit visor modules at price parity** ($449 / $749), co-developed and snag-validated with one helmet maker (Cookie, Bonehead or Tonfly), and offer a co-branded helmet bundle through that partner. Defer a full certified smart helmet until more than 3,000 pods are in the field and a helmet OEM will share the certification burden. The product should launch as advisory-only, baro+IMU-fused, camera-free and restricted to B-licence holders and above, and it should meet the PLD, battery-removability and RED cybersecurity requirements from day one. Plan the company around roughly $1M in total funding, a team of two to four people, and a dealer-plus-DTC mix. Keep the Dropzone tier and a paragliding/speedflying firmware variant as the two main growth options.

| Kill or pivot criterion | Threshold | Decision point |
|---|---|---|
| Sunlight readability and fit | Display unreadable in direct sun at altitude, or fogging/fit failures inside 3 major helmet models | Feb 2027 (before PIA) |
| Altitude accuracy | Baro+IMU altitude error > ±30 m versus reference, or latency > 250 ms, across 200+ test jumps | Q2 2027 |
| Demand | Fewer than **250 refundable deposits** (Recon's threshold) and fewer than **5 dealer LOIs** within 90 days of PIA 2027 | May 2027 |
| Cost | Standard landed COGS > $200 or premium > $330 at 1,000 units (DTC margin < 55%) | DVT quote, Q3 2027 |
| Liability | No product-liability and excess cover available at a cost the plan can absorb | Before PVT order |
| Governing-body pushback | Formal S&TA/DZO or federation objection to HUD use by licensed jumpers at target hubs | Rolling |
| Subscription | Pro attach < 10% of owners after 12 months → collapse into a free app plus a Coach/DZ B2B tier only | 2029 |
| Helmet module | No helmet OEM willing to co-validate a mount → glasses-only line | End of 2027 |

The validation plan runs in sequence, and each step is cheap relative to NRE. From now to January 2027, build a works-like rig from an off-the-shelf HUD module and a baro/IMU/GNSS board. Complete 30–50 structured interviews with wingsuit, canopy and camera flyers, three to five dealer interviews on discount and stocking terms, and five dropzone-owner conversations testing the Dropzone tier. Obtain the USPA survey PDF to confirm the licence distribution. From January to February 2027, run sunlight, fogging and latency tests on 50+ jumps with two sponsored coaches, and bring the rig to PIA with a human-factors seminar. From March to June 2027, run a closed beta of 30–50 jumpers and 1,000+ logged jumps with an instructor safety panel. Collect deposits and LOIs, get liability-insurance and CM quotes, and commit to DVT only if the demand, cost and insurance gates pass.

## Conclusion

The real constraint on this business is not technology or even price. It is that the addressable population is small and shrinking, and a series of failures has taught it not to trust pre-orders. A product that simply ships, keeps working, and is honest about being advisory therefore has a competitive advantage that is worth more than any display feature. That changes the strategic question from "which four SKUs?" to "how few things must work perfectly?" The answer is one pod, two tiers, glasses first, and helmets through partners.

The subscription model does not change the economics. At skydiving scale, membership revenue stays in the tens of thousands of dollars a year and brings Garmin-style backlash risk, so its real job is retention and data. The more promising recurring line is B2B: dropzones and coaches who can use aggregated jump data to run safer, better-instructed operations. If the dropzone tier shows traction during validation, it will likely be a better route to scale than any extra hardware SKU. Paragliding and speedflying are the other option for reaching a larger market without a new product.
